Unearthing the Roots
of a Stubborn Tree

How I confronted procrastination by building structure, running at dawn, and learning that it takes time to unearth the roots of a burrowed and stubborn tree.

Explanation — Not the Final Deliverable

Read the Explanation
The Problem

Six items due Sunday.
I'd start Sunday.

My problem was never that I couldn't do the work. It was that I wouldn't start it. If I had six items due on Sunday, the rational thing would be to spread them across the week — chip away Monday through Saturday, then finish strong. Instead, I'd wait. Sunday would arrive, and I'd do all six in a single, miserable sitting.

Procrastination had burrowed into my workflow like a stubborn root. It wasn't laziness exactly — it was a resistance to beginning, a loop of avoidance that fed on itself. The longer I waited, the heavier the work became, and the heavier it became, the more I wanted to wait.

I knew my worth. I knew I was capable. But capability doesn't matter if you can't sit down and begin. I needed three things:

Better planning — a structure that made starting automatic, not optional
Less procrastination — breaking the cycle of delay and last-minute panic
Confidence in my abilities — trusting that if I start, I can finish

The Setback

15 minutes became 48.
My grade became 50%.

In the early weeks of class, I wasn't attending. I had just recently moved from Chandler, AZ to Laveen, AZ, and the change hit harder than I expected.

My old commute from Chandler to ASU Polytechnic was 15 minutes — easy, automatic, barely a thought. From Laveen, it was 48 minutes. That extra 33 minutes each way became an excuse. The drive felt monumental, and my procrastination used it as fuel. "It's too far," I'd tell myself. "I'll just miss today."

I accumulated 4 absences. My grade slid down to 50%. The stubborn tree wasn't just burrowed — it was actively growing, feeding on my unwillingness to change.

The conversation that changed it

The shift started with communication. I talked to my professor. I was honest about the move, the commute, and my own resistance to adapting. That conversation was the first crack in the wall. By curbing my procrastination and forcing myself to accept the new reality of the drive, I began to climb back. I brought my grade up from 50% to where it is now. It wasn't instant, and it wasn't easy — but acknowledging the problem out loud made it real, and making it real made it solvable.

The Turning Point

MEPS & the Morning Run

February 9th changed my rhythm. What started as a military processing requirement became the anchor of every day that followed.

February 9, 2025

On February 9th, I attended MEPS — Military Entrance Processing Station. That experience forced me to confront my physical readiness and, unexpectedly, my mental readiness too. I came away with a decision: I was going to run.

The Linear Day

Before running, my day had no spine. I'd wake up and the day was shapeless — a blob of time that I'd fill reactively. Procrastination thrives in shapeless time.

Running changed everything. When I wake up and run first thing, my day becomes linear:

Wake up Run Shower Go

There's no decision to make. No negotiation with myself. The run happens, and then the day happens. The momentum from the run carries me into the next task, and the next. It's a domino effect that starts with lacing up my shoes.

Why Running Works (For Procrastination)

  • Eliminates the first decision. The hardest part of procrastination is starting. Running removes that — you just go.
  • Creates physiological momentum. After a run, your body is awake. Your mind is awake. You're already in motion — it's easier to stay in motion.
  • Builds a success frame. You've already accomplished something before 10am. That changes how you approach everything else.
  • Structures the day. Once the run is done, the rest of the schedule naturally falls into place around it.

What the Schedule Revealed

Looking at the full week, I noticed something: running appears every single day. That wasn't an accident. Once I committed to it, the run became the fixed point that everything else rotated around. The class times were set. The library blocks filled in around them. Homework slotted into the gaps. Even rest and game time had their place — because the schedule made room for them instead of letting them expand to fill the whole day.

The schedule didn't eliminate procrastination overnight. But it made it visible. When I looked at the plan and then looked at what I was actually doing, the gap between intention and action became obvious — and that awareness was the first step toward closing it.

The Journey

From Concept to Check-Ins

This project unfolded in steps. Each one forced me to articulate what I was trying to do, test it, and be honest about whether it was working.

Step 1 — Initial Concept

Identifying the Problem

I brainstormed what was holding me back. The answer was clear: I couldn't plan, I procrastinated constantly, and I lacked confidence in my own workflow. I set a goal — to be efficient and to know my worth — and proposed testing a daily schedule and planning initiative.

Step 2 — Project Plan

Designing the Test

I committed to three testable actions: (1) Run and do homework — linking physical exertion to mental focus, (2) After homework, rest the body and mind — not collapsing, but intentional recovery, (3) Good sleep — protecting the foundation everything else sits on. I also identified a reminder app as a tool, and set my final deadline for May 1st, 2026.

Step 3 — First Check-In

Habits Starting to Harden

By the first check-in, my plan was coming together — the schedule was becoming a straight path, not a tangled one. But during the Speed Sharing activity, I noticed something: my peers had multiple goals and intrusive thoughts about what they wanted to achieve. I was lacking in that area. I still needed to look deeper into myself. The structure was forming, but the self-awareness was still a work in progress.

Step 4 — Final Check-In

Two Tasks Remaining

By the final check-in, I had two things left to work on. First: making a daily schedule. My weekly to-do list was mostly consistent, but there were still day-to-day variations that threw me off. Second: knowing what I can and can't do. I was still trying to do things I knew were beyond my capacity, setting myself up for failure and then procrastinating to avoid the frustration. I decided my final deliverable would be a journal — a record of progress from the start of class to now.

What I Tested & Measured

Running → Homework pipeline Effective

Running first made starting homework significantly easier. The momentum carried over.

Intentional rest after work Helpful

When I rested intentionally (not collapsed), I had more energy the next day. Hard to maintain consistently.

Good sleep discipline Still improving

Sleep is the foundation, but late-night gaming still cuts into it. This is the next root to pull.

The Reminder App

I used a reminder app to push notifications for tasks and commitments. It worked — I started seeing results quickly. But I also realized the app alone wasn't enough. A notification can remind you to start, but it can't make you start. That's where the run came in. The run eliminates the need for willpower because it's not a decision — it's just what happens at 9am. The app tells me what to do. The run gets me doing.

As I wrote in my plan: "The reminder app I used is great, and I already see results. But I think it can be better." The run was that "better."

Reflection

It Takes Time to Unearth the Roots

Procrastination has gotten better. That's the honest truth. It has gotten better. But there is still a lot of work to be done. I'm not going to pretend I've solved it — I haven't. Some weeks the schedule holds. Some weeks it doesn't. Some mornings I run and feel like the day is mine. Other mornings I don't, and the whole day feels like I'm swimming upstream.

The metaphor that keeps coming back to me is the one I opened with: it takes time to unearth the roots of a burrowed and stubborn tree. Procrastination isn't a weed you pull once. It's a tree with deep roots. You can cut it back, you can weaken it, you can make it smaller — but those roots are still there, and if you stop digging, it grows back.

The schedule is my shovel. The run is my first strike each morning. The reminder app, the homework blocks, the intentional rest — those are the follow-through swings. Each one loosens the roots a little more. Each day I follow the schedule, the tree gets weaker.

I think the most important thing I've learned is that structure doesn't restrict freedom — it creates it. When my day is planned, I don't spend energy deciding what to do. I just do it. The decision has already been made. And that's the whole point: procrastination lives in the space between not-doing and doing. Structure closes that space.

The move to Laveen nearly broke me early on. A 48-minute commute felt like a wall I couldn't climb, and I let it become an excuse to stop showing up. But communicating with my professor — just being honest about what was happening — was the first real step forward. That conversation didn't fix my grade overnight, but it stopped the bleeding. It reminded me that I'm not alone in this, and that unwillingness to change is just another form of procrastination. Once I accepted the new drive and built my schedule around it instead of against it, everything started to shift.

Where I Still Need Work

Sleep discipline. Late-night gaming still eats into sleep, which undercuts the next day's run and schedule. This is the next root to pull.
Knowing my limits. I still try to do things I know I can't reasonably accomplish, then feel defeated and procrastinate. I need to get honest about capacity.
Self-inquiry. My peers had goals that came from within. Mine felt vague. I need to keep looking into myself — not just at my schedule, but at what I actually want and why I avoid starting.

My goals for this project were simple but serious: I want a permanent impact. If I can build these habits now — the running, the scheduling, the starting-before-I'm-ready — they'll carry me through my working adult life. And I want a healthier life. The exercise routine isn't just about fighting procrastination; it's about longevity. It's about being someone who runs, who plans, who starts.

The tree is still standing. But the roots are loosening. And I'm still digging.
